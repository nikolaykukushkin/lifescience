From the final exam · bonus question

What do you think is the smallest animal that can knock you over?

Knocking over means the animal, by its own action, uses its body to push or pull you off balance so that you fall to the ground.

Thirty-seven students answered, with guesses spanning six orders of magnitude — from a 100 mg bee to a 200 kg pony. The range covers raw force, momentum, grip, swarms, and a few takes that get us arguing about what "push or pull" really means. Below: where they all landed.

Current winner

Perhaps this Brazilian rainbow boa.

The class median lands at 2 kg of snake. The most honest behavioral fit at that weight is a small boa — and a Brazilian rainbow boa (Epicrates cenchria) is a great candidate. Iridescent, semi-arboreal, and engaged enough to wrap a limb when startled. A ball python at the same weight tends to coil into a defensive ball; a small boa actually does something.

Could it knock you over? On flat ground, a 2 kg snake doesn't have the mass or torque to push or pull a standing adult off balance. But a small boa wrapped around your ankle mid-stride could absolutely bring you down — your weight transfers to that foot, you can't lift it, and you go through your own momentum.

Whether that's really "pushing or pulling" is a fun question. The snake's body is essentially an anchor — your own walking does the work. Not quite the active force the question asks for, but the closest a 2 kg snake gets to the right physics.

By taxonomic class
Where each group landed.

Mammals are the largest category.

14 answers · 2 g – 200 kg

And probably the most intuitive — animals you can imagine actually being tackled by. Lots of dogs and dog-adjacent mammals (rottweilers, golden retrievers), plus a wombat, a ram, a boar, a large pig, monkeys, a beaver, and a pony at the heavy end. Two mice represent the other extreme: the smallest mammalian mass that could still pull this off.

Birds bet on momentum.

7 answers · 5 g – 5 kg

The peregrine, falcon, hawk, and eagle all converge on the same strategy — convert altitude or speed into impact. The hummingbird goes the other way, reaching for the smallest possible flyer. One student just answered "a bird," keeping things general.

Snakes spread across two orders of magnitude.

4 answers · 0.5 kg – 50 kg

The shared insight is right: snakes can use weight transfer, grip, and constriction to topple a person, even at modest sizes. The disagreement is about scale — from a half-kilo arboreal snake at the small end to a 50 kg anaconda at the other. The 2 kg "snake" answer also sits exactly at the class median (see above).

Arthropods go small and creative.

9 answers · 0.1 g – 5 kg

The small-mass strategy: nine answers — bees, butterflies, cockroaches, scorpions, ant colonies, a mantis shrimp, a spider, and a coconut crab — betting that a tiny creature with the right setup could pull it off through stings, swarms, weight underfoot, or grip. Some depend on bites or stings (mechanisms the rules exclude), but the coconut crab is the standout: with enough grip strength to yank a leg out from under you, it's a fully mechanical answer at just 4 kg.
